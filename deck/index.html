The most successful synthesis method nobody could program.

Forty years of FM synthesis, and the recurring problem was never the sound. It was the interface to the parameter space.

Discovery1967 · Stanford AI Lab

Push vibrato past hearing, and it stops being movement.

John Chowning drove a vibrato faster and faster. Past roughly 20 Hz the ear can no longer follow it — and the wobble collapses into a new timbre.

Why it matteredTwo oscillators

One number, and a whole spectrum appears.

Barely any computation, and timbres no analog synth could reach. Stanford licensed it to Yamaha.

Mass market1983 · Yamaha DX7
200,000+units sold · first affordable digital synth

If you have heard anything recorded in the eighties, you have heard this instrument.

The problemOne parameter at a time

You could never see the patch. Only the parameter you stood on.

Six operators. Thirty-two algorithms. Around 150 parameters — behind two lines of display.

ConsequencePreset culture

So it became a preset machine.

The presets were excellent, and they were the ceiling. An instrument that could make almost any timbre, mostly playing back thirty-two of them.

Software eraNative Instruments FM8

More screen. Same prerequisite.

Everything visible at once, a full modulation matrix — and still an instrument that assumes you already know what a modulation index does.

The pattern1983 → 2019

Every era fixed the interface. None of them moved the expertise.

  • 1983DX7 — a window onto one parameter
  • 2006FM8 — the whole matrix on screen
  • 2019Quad Operator — knobs, CV, playable
Claudio2026

Don't move the knobs. Move the expertise.

Drop in any sound — it needn't be an FM sound — or just say “a dark rubbery bass”. Something that already knows FM does the programming.

How it worksClosed measurement loop

It never hears anything. It measures.

Propose, render, analyse, compare, correct. The feedback is numeric — “harmonic 7 is 30 dB too quiet” — so each pass is an experiment, not a guess.
